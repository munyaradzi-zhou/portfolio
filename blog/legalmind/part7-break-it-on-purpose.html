Break it on purpose

LegalMind on Azure · Part 7

TL;DR — I wrote five alerts and a runbook, then broke the system on purpose to see whether they worked. All five alerts fired, and the drills found four problems: one alert could never have fired, and the runbook's fix for a lost permission didn't fix anything. Then I put API Management in front of the model as an AI gateway, load-tested it up to 30 users and throttled the primary model region in the middle of it: 160 calls moved to the second region and users saw no errors. The semantic cache I expected to save money saved little and reused answers across different questions, so it's off. An answer costs under a cent; two thirds of the bill is the search service, whether anyone asks anything or not.

Part 6 was about catching bad answers before they ship. This one is about knowing when the running system is in trouble, and what happens when it is.

Five alerts, and a drill for each

The alerts are in Bicep, next to everything else:

Alert Fires when
API 5xx rate more than 2% of requests fail in 5 minutes
Chat latency p95 of answers above 20 s over 15 minutes
Unverified answers more than 20% of answers in an hour couldn't be checked (the fail-closed label from Part 2)
Model throttling Azure OpenAI answers 429
Container restarts the API container restarts

The third one is the interesting one. Uptime alerts tell you the system is down; this one tells you it's up and wrong: answering, but without the check that makes the answers trustworthy.

A runbook says what to do for each. Then the drills, on dev, on purpose:

  • Throttle the model: its capacity cut from 30K to 1K tokens a minute. Throttling, latency and unverified-answer alerts all fired. Every answer during the drill was labelled unverified, not passed off as checked: the guard from Part 2 doing its job under stress.
  • Kill the process: the restart alert fired three and a half minutes later.
  • Remove a permission: the app's identity lost its role on the search index. Every search and chat failed; the 5xx alert fired seven minutes after the first error.

What the drills found

  1. The quality alert could never have fired. The grounding label was attached to an internal FastAPI span that never reaches the requests table the alert queries. It looked fine in the code and in every unit test. Fixed, with a test for where the attribute lands.
  2. The runbook's fix didn't work. For a lost role it said "redeploy the infrastructure". I did, and nothing changed: the deploy script only re-applies the Bicep when infra/ has changed in git, which is a good rule for fast releases and a bad one for repairing drift. A --reapply-infra option, also on the CD workflow, restored the role. Resolved 22 minutes after the first error, on the second attempt.
  3. The alert that fired before the drill. Throttling fired three minutes before I'd throttled anything: an evaluation run (Part 6) was using the same model deployment as the app. A quality gate shouldn't be able to slow down users.
  4. Restarts are hard to cause. A revision restart creates new replicas, not a restart; a SIGKILL to PID 1 from inside the container is ignored by the kernel. SIGTERM works. Now in the runbook.

An AI gateway

Finding 3 was the real lesson: one model deployment was the ceiling for everything. So API Management now sits between the app and Azure OpenAI:

  • Only the app may call it (its managed identity's token is checked); the gateway calls the models with its own identity.
  • Per-user token limits: one user, or one runaway agent loop, can't use everyone's capacity. The app tells the gateway who each call is for.
  • Token metrics per user in Application Insights.
  • Failover: a pool with the primary region (East US 2) first and a second account in France Central behind it. A circuit breaker takes a throttled or failing backend out of the pool, and a retry sends the call to the next one.
  • Evaluations got their own deployment, so finding 3 can't happen again.

The existing API Management instance couldn't do any of this: the Consumption tier supports neither token limits nor circuit breakers, and can't be upgraded in place. The new instance is the Developer tier, at about €1.50 a day. Production would use Standard v2.

The load test

Locust, simulated lawyers asking questions from the golden set, ramped in stages:

users answers/min p50 p95 errors API replicas
2 7 7.4 s 14.9 s 0% 1
5 20 7.2 s 9.6 s 0% 1
10 32 7.6 s 18.3 s 0% 1
20 29 29.0 s 53.5 s 0% 1 → 2
30 29 54.0 s 86.8 s 0% 2 → 3

No errors at any stage, and a ceiling at about 30 answers a minute: after that, more users only wait longer. The API's CPU stayed low. The ceiling is the models: an answer makes about five model calls, roughly 15K tokens, and 30 answers a minute is the combined capacity of both regions. Adding replicas wouldn't help; adding model capacity would, and on Global Standard reserving capacity costs nothing until it's used.

I did tune one thing. The circuit breaker took a backend out for a full minute after a single 429, so both regions were often out at once (511 calls got a 503 from the gateway, which the app's retries absorbed). Three failures in ten seconds, out for ten seconds, made no measurable difference: when both regions are full, it doesn't matter which one you wait for.

Taking a region down

The drill: cut East US 2's capacity to 1K tokens a minute and run the load.

The first attempt measured nothing: all 199 calls succeeded on East US 2. A capacity change takes about six minutes to be enforced. The second time, a probe waited for the first 429 before the load started. Then:

calls
East US 2: answered / throttled 9 / 22
France Central: answered 160
Answers to users 36, 0 errors

Losing the primary region didn't reach users. The price was latency: p95 went from about 9 s to 37 s, for the calls that hit the throttled region first and waited for the retry.

The gateway has its own failure mode, too: the Developer tier, one unit and no SLA, was unreachable for about eight minutes that same day. That's the trade you make for €1.50 a day.

The cache that didn't pay

The gateway can also cache: a semantic cache in Redis that answers a model call from a stored one when the prompt is similar enough. I partitioned it by what the caller may see (Part 6), so a cached answer can never reach someone without access to its sources. Then I measured: twenty questions, asked twice.

  • On the exact repeat, 18% of model calls came from the cache, and answers weren't faster.
  • On the first pass, 7% came from the cache too, before any question had been asked twice: similar-looking prompts from different questions were treated as the same.

Most of LegalMind's model calls carry eight retrieved passages and the agent's tool steps, so they rarely match. The ones that do are what you'd least want reused. Redis costs €1–2 a day. Off.

The other outage

One more failure, not on purpose. On the morning of the load test, every search and chat on dev started answering 500. The logs: "Free Query Semantic Usage exceeded for the month." The semantic ranker's free plan allows 1,000 queries a month, and three weeks of evaluations had used them. Every search asked for the ranker, so every search failed. Search now falls back to plain hybrid search for an hour at a time when that happens. (The evaluation gate later measured what the ranker is worth: top-3 retrieval dropped from 0.88 to 0.75 without it.)

What it costs

From Cost Management and the token counts of 657 real answers:

  • Dev at rest: €2.33 a day, about €70 a month. Two thirds of it is AI Search (Basic), billed per hour whether anyone asks anything or not. The gateway adds about €1.50 a day.
  • An answer: about €0.0085: 3.3 model calls, 10,100 input and 490 output tokens (most of it the retrieved passages), plus the safety checks. At 1,000 answers a day that's €8, still less than the fixed costs.

Takeaways

  • Break it on purpose. Two of the four findings were things that looked right in code: an alert that couldn't fire, and a runbook step that didn't repair anything.
  • Alert on quality, not just uptime: "up and wrong" is the failure a legal assistant can't afford.
  • Know your real ceiling before adding replicas: here it was model capacity, which is free to raise.
  • In a drill, prove the fault is in place before you measure the response.
  • Measure the optimisation before you keep it. The cache's best case was 18%, and its worst case was reusing an answer across questions.
  • Look at where the money goes at rest. Per-answer costs were the small part.
